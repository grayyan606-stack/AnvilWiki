## What is the main objective?

Your immediate objective is to bring eggs back to your base. Each successful return creates another chance to hatch a useful pet. Pets generate money, money funds upgrades, and speed gives you access to more difficult areas and safer returns.

## The six-step loop

1. **Choose an egg route.** Start where you can reliably escape rather than chasing the rarest visible egg.
2. **Steal one egg.** Watch the guardian and nearby players before committing.
3. **Return to base.** Carrying a larger or more contested egg can make the route harder.
4. **Hatch the egg.** Check the resulting pet's base income, size, and mutation.
5. **Upgrade income and capacity.** Replace weak slots only when the new pet actually improves the total.
6. **Train speed.** Measure your gain rate and work toward the next biome with a safety margin.

<Callout type="tip" title="Progression rule">A safe mid-value return is usually better than repeatedly losing the highest-value egg you can barely reach.</Callout>

Use the [egg guide](/eggs/egg-guide), [pet index](/pets/all-pets), and [speed calculator](/tools/calculators#speed-calculator) as you move through the loop.

## What should a new player learn before chasing a rare hatch?

Learn to recognize your base, the path back to it, and the controls shown by the current game interface. The official experience description supports several device types, so a keyboard instruction copied from another player's video may not match your screen. Use the prompt you can actually see instead of assuming a universal key binding.

Your first useful milestone is one complete cycle that you understand. Identify an egg, return it, follow the visible hatch interaction, and inspect the resulting pet. Pay attention to which action starts each step. A clear ordinary success teaches more about the loop than reaching a distant target without knowing what to do next.

Avoid changing every system immediately. If you alter the base, switch pets, and begin training at the same time, you may not know which action improved the next result. Give each change a purpose: improve a slot, learn a route, or reach a measured movement goal.

## How do you decide what to do next?

Identify the step that currently prevents another useful return. That is your immediate bottleneck. It may be navigation, movement, route pressure, or the fact that new hatches no longer improve your occupied slots. Different bottlenecks need different responses.

| What you notice | A useful next action |
| --- | --- |
| You get lost on the return | Practice the route and identify reliable landmarks |
| You can enter an area but rarely return | Test a shorter route and measure carrying performance |
| A new pet replaces nothing useful | Compare its displayed contribution with your weakest occupied slot |
| The next route needs more movement progress | Measure your current training gain and set a specific target |
| Several attempts fail for different reasons | Log the reasons before assuming one upgrade solves them all |

These are decision rules, not a prescribed purchase order. The live base interface determines which upgrades are available and what they cost. Inspect that information before spending in-game currency, and choose the upgrade whose effect addresses the problem you have observed.

## What does pet income tell you about progress?

An income card helps compare contributions, but first determine whether the number is a base value or a final value with modifiers included. Keep the same interpretation for all pets you compare. Otherwise, a pet entered into a calculator with its effects counted twice can look better on paper than it is in the game.

For a hypothetical example, replacing a contribution of 5 units per second with 9 adds 4 units per second to a comparable setup. The new pet's full 9 is not the improvement because you removed an existing contribution. Filling a genuinely unused active slot would have a different effect. These values illustrate replacement arithmetic and are not data for any named pet.

Use the [income calculator](/tools/income-calculator) when you know the input definitions. Use the [pet index](/pets/all-pets) for navigation and evidence context. Neither tool replaces checking the card and total shown in your own current base.

## How does training fit into the loop?

The official description identifies treadmill training as a way to gain speed. Your planning task is to turn that activity into a measurable target. Record your starting speed, train for a known duration without changing the setup, and record the ending speed. Divide the gain by elapsed seconds to estimate your rate for those conditions.

That estimate lets you compare a training goal with the time you have available. It does not prove that the same rate will hold after an equipment change, an event, or an update. If the observed rate changes, update the input rather than forcing the new session to fit the old estimate.

Reaching a gate and completing a return are separate tests. After a movement upgrade, try an ordinary route before assuming the largest or most contested target is now comfortable. The [speed calculator](/tools/speed-calculator) supports the training estimate; a real carrying run checks whether the improvement solves your route problem.

## How should you respond to a failed run?

Record the main reason while it is still clear. Missing a turn calls for route practice. Waiting too long at a contested target calls for a different timing or target choice. A repeated inability to complete the return may justify more training or a shorter route. Labeling every loss “bad luck” prevents you from seeing the part you can change.

A failed run also used time. Include that time when comparing routes instead of timing only your fastest success. If you want a structured comparison, the [route profitability field test](/guides/route-profitability-field-test) uses equal session lengths and records all attempts.

Keep a collection session separate from a progression session. Exploring a new area or seeking a particular hatch can be enjoyable even when it is not the fastest route to a higher base total. Naming your goal helps you evaluate the session by the result you actually wanted.

## How do you know a session made useful progress?

End with a small record: one completed route you understand, your current displayed income, your measured training rate, and one next action. You do not need a rare hatch for the session to have value. Better route knowledge, a reliable baseline, or a clear explanation for a failure can make the next session more productive.
